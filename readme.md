# Time-Frequency Analysis: Empirical Fourier Transformation: A Precise Signal Decomposition Method for Nonlinear and Nonstationary Time Series Analysis [with MATLAB Code]

Introduction: Signal decomposition is a numerical tool extensively applied in various fields such as biomedical signal analysis [1], seismic signal analysis [2], mechanical vibration signal analysis [3,4], and speech enhancement [5]. Time-domain signals from physical systems are typically composed of several superimposed components, referred to as modes. These modes can contain meaningful frequency domain information about the signal, known as mode information. Therefore, obtaining high-precision and efficient signal decomposition results is crucial.
Over the past few decades, various signal decomposition methods have been developed. Among them, Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) is one of the most important methods. Although its mathematical principles are not yet clear, and there are known deficiencies such as mode mixing [8] and endpoint effects [9]. To overcome these deficiencies, an improved version of EMD has been developed. Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (EEMD) [10] alleviates modal mixing and endpoint effects by adding limited amplitude white noise. Complete Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (CEEMD) [11] further improves EMD by adding completeness and fully data-driven mode number (which is missing in EEMD). Zheng et al. [12] introduced the arrangement entropy into partial set empirical mode decomposition to quantify the interruption and noise in signals, compared with EEMD and CEEMD, the method has fewer pseudo components in the decomposition results. Lang et al. [13] replaced the mean operator with a median operator in EEMD to resolve the mode splitting issue in EEMD; Li et al. proposed time-varying filtering technology to address mode mixing issues [15]. However, these EMD methods cannot fundamentally solve the problems of mode mixing and endpoint effects. Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) [16] is a non-recursive signal decomposition method based on the extension of Wiener filter. Recently, the VMD has been improved by adding several criteria to ensure that the target mode does not overlap or is less overlapped with other modes in the decomposition results [17]. The criteria added in the continuous VMD can eliminate the requirement for predefined signal components to be decomposed in the VMD [16]. Furthermore, to avoid the failure of VMD in processing non-stationary time-domain signals with linear frequency modulation modes, McNeill [18] proposed an optimized objective function with short-time narrowband mode constraints. Chen et al. [19] then extended VMD using a fully variational framework. Empirical Wavelet Transform (EWT) employs an adaptive wavelet filter bank based on the Fourier spectral decomposition [20]. References [21-23] Improved the practicality of EWT to eliminate its requirement for high signal-to-noise ratio; Enhanced EWT through Fourier-Bessel series expansion to improve time-frequency representations (TFRs) of non-stationary signals [24]. However, the transitional phases between the filter functions of EWT wavelet filters group lead to modal mixing problems when dealing with signals containing dense modes. Additionally, trivial component issues may arise, where the first component of EWT decomposition corresponds to meaningless residuals. Due to the inclusion of trivial decomposition components, it is difficult to determine the number of predefined components to be decomposed in the signal. The Fourier Transform Method (FDM) [25] is an adaptive non-stationary and nonlinear signal decomposition method based on the Fourier theory and Hilbert transform, which decomposes a zero-mean signal into a set of Fourier eigenfunctions (FIBFs). FDM has several limitations and two frequency scanning techniques have been developed to obtain FIBF: one is called low to high (LTH) technique, the other is called high to low (HTL) technique. LTH and HTL techniques respectively estimate FIBFs through forward and reverse recursion. However, the decomposition results obtained by FDM using these two frequency scanning technologies (i.e., FDM-HTL and FDM-LTH) may be inconsistent and it is impossible to determine which decomposition result is correct. In addition, both of these frequency scanning technologies are iterative, resulting in longer computation times for FDM. Recently, the application of Discrete Cosine Transform (DCTs), non-causal infinite impulse response filters and finite impulse response filters has been introduced to extend FDM [26,27]. For example, the FDM-DCT based on DCTs obtains several FIBFs with predefined cutoff frequencies using DCTs and improves the time-frequency representation accuracy of FDM [27].
This paper briefly reviews EWT and FDM, describes the segmentation technique in EWT and the construction of wavelet filter bank in EWT, as well as the construction of FIBFs in FDM and two frequency scanning techniques. A new adaptive signal decomposition method - Experienced Fourier Decomposition (EFD) is proposed to address the problems existing in EWT and FDM. The main contributions of EFD are: (1) it can simultaneously solve the trivial component problem and mode mixing problem in the decomposition results of EWT; (2) it can solve the inconsistency problem in the decomposition results of FDM. The significance of these contributions stems from the improved segmentation technique combined with zero-phase filter bank. Through numerical studies, the accuracy of EFD compared with EMD, VMD, EWT and FDM on decomposing two non-stationary signals and two signals containing dense modes is demonstrated; moreover, the precision of time-frequency representation and computational efficiency of EFD compared with other methods are also presented. The effectiveness of EFD on real measured signals containing dense modes is verified through experiments.
The rest of this paper is arranged as follows: Section 2 briefly reviews EWT and FDM; Section 3 describes the proposed EFD; Section 4 presents the numerical study results; Section 5 presents experimental study results; Section 6 gives conclusions and discusses future work.
Conclusion: This paper proposes an accurate and efficient EFD method for decomposing time-domain signals. The EFD includes two key steps: the development of an improved segmentation technique, and the construction of a zero-phase filter bank. In the improved segmentation technique, an adaptive sorting process is developed to obtain precise segmentation results and eliminate the trivial residue that may exist in the first decomposition component (corresponding to the trivial component problem in EWT). The use of zero-phase filters helps in eliminating transitional phases that may cause mode mixing issues in EWT, facilitating the precise decomposition of signals with dense modes. The segmentation technique addresses the inconsistency issue in FDM by predefining the number of components to be decomposed in the signal. Numerical studies on non-stationary signals show that EFD can provide high-precision and consistent decomposition results. A numerical study on two stationary signals is used to analyze the decomposition performance of EFD on dense modes, indicating that EFD has high precision and consistency in decomposing dense modes, and its decomposition results are more precise than those of other decomposition methods. Additionally, EFD provides precise time-frequency representations for non-stationary signals. Comparative computational times between EFD, EWT, FDM, VMD, and EMD indicate that EFD has the highest computational efficiency. Experimental validation using real-world response measurements from benchmark problems shows that EFD can provide the most accurate decomposition results compared to other decomposition methods.
However, one limitation of EFD is that it cannot precisely decompose signals with cross-component components in the frequency domain, due to the improved segmentation method's inability to distinguish these components at the moment of crossover in their frequency domains. Future work could explore the applicability of EFD in higher-dimensional signals / data, such as digital images and vibration measurement data from continuous scanning laser Doppler vibrometers.
